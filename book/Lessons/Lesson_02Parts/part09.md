# 9: Acid/base Catalysis \& Brønsted Plots



Catalysts **change** the **rate** of a reaction without being consumed during the process. Small amounts of catalyst can change the outcome of a reaction by making one result much **faster** than other possibilities. Using a catalyst we can favour one enantioner over another in a reaction, making them an important and lucrative field of chemistry. In biochemistry you learned that enzymes are the catalysts that define life itself. Glucose and ATP in water will produce no change in the glucose. After a **few days** we will observe glucose and hydrolysis products of ATP. But if the enzyme *hexokinase* is present, we will so only glucose-6-phosphate **rapidly** produced. Both reactions are exothermic, but we catalyzed only one possibility and changed the outcome.

Today we will explore just one kind of catalysis, **acid-base catalysis**.

The **acid** in an acid **catalyzed** reaction **transfers a proton** to the reactant. What if we **change the acid**? Let us imagine a whole series of acids with different $pK_a$ values. Would the **rate constant** for catalysis **change** in proportion to the $pK_a$ values? It might or it might not, depending on the **mechanism**. This will be our first example of using **structure-activity relationships** to interpret reaction mechansims. Today we introduce the **Brønsted plot**.

The $pK_a$ of an acid is a **function** of the acid dissociation constant, $K_a$. The value of $K_a$ ia a function of the **free-energy difference** for the proton transfer to water. If we plot $\log{k}$ *vs* $pK_a$ we are plotting the free-energy of activation against the free-energy of the proton transfer.
